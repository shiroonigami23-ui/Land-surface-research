India's Land: Six Physical Divisions

From the Himalaya to the Thar, the Indo-Gangetic Plain, the Deccan, the coasts and the islands, a tour of India's land, soils, rainfall and the crops each region grows.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 3 min read🔗 2 sources🎓 Introductory

India covers 3.287 million km², about 2.4% of the world’s land, yet supports roughly 17–18% of its people. That is possible because of fertile river plains, a monsoon that delivers most of the year’s water in four months, and soils that range from deep alluvium to basalt-derived black earth.

India at a glance
  • Highest point: Kangchenjunga, 8,586 m (Sikkim)
  • Lowest point: Kuttanad, Kerala, about −2.2 m (farmed below sea level)
  • Wettest places: Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, Meghalaya, ~11,800 mm a year
  • Driest: western Rajasthan and Ladakh, under 100 mm in places
  • Net sown area: about 140 million hectares, roughly 43% of the land, among the highest shares of any large country

1. The Himalaya and the northern mountains#

A 2,400 km arc of fold mountains in three parallel ranges: the Greater Himalaya (Himadri) with peaks over 6,000 m, the Lesser Himalaya (Himachal) with hill stations such as Shimla and Mussoorie, and the Shiwaliks foothills, whose flat valleys are called duns (Dehradun). Beyond lie the cold deserts of Ladakh and the Karakoram. The Himalaya’s glaciers feed the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra. Crops: apples, cherries and walnuts (Kashmir, Himachal), tea (Darjeeling), rice terraces, barley and buckwheat at altitude; saffron at Pampore in Kashmir.

The Northeast hills (Patkai, Naga, Mizo, Garo–Khasi–Jaintia) are among the wettest and most biodiverse parts of India, with jhum (shifting) cultivation, tea in Assam’s Brahmaputra valley and the living root bridges of Meghalaya.

2. The Indo-Gangetic–Brahmaputra Plain#

One of the largest and most fertile alluvial plains on Earth, formed by Himalayan rivers dumping sediment into a trough in front of the mountains. Subregions: the bhabar (pebbly foot of the hills, where streams vanish underground), the terai (marshy, now largely cleared), bhangar (older alluvium with lime nodules, kankar) and khadar (new flood-renewed alluvium). This is India’s grain bowl: wheat and rice in Punjab and Haryana, sugarcane in western UP, rice and jute in Bihar, West Bengal and Assam.

3. The Thar Desert#

Western Rajasthan’s sand dunes and rocky plains, with 100–500 mm of rain. See Deserts of the world. Crops: bajra, guar, moth bean, mustard and wheat where canals reach.

4. The Peninsular Plateau#

India’s oldest land, a block of ancient crystalline rock (part of Gondwana) bordered by the Aravalli, Vindhya and Satpura ranges and the Western and Eastern Ghats. It contains: - the Central Highlands: Malwa plateau (black soil, soybeans, wheat), Bundelkhand (drought-prone), Chota Nagpur (minerals, red soils, tribal forests); - the Deccan Plateau: black cotton soil (regur) in Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh grows cotton, soybeans, sorghum and pulses; red and laterite soils in Karnataka, Telangana and Tamil Nadu grow millets, groundnut and pulses.

5. The coastal plains#

  • West coast: narrow and wet: the Konkan, Goa and the Malabar coast of Kerala, with lagoons and backwaters; rice, coconut, cashew, pepper, cardamom and rubber.
  • East coast: broad, with the great deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri, the rice bowls of Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Exposed to Bay of Bengal cyclones.

6. The islands#

  • Andaman and Nicobar Islands: a chain of submerged mountains in the Bay of Bengal, mostly rainforest, with India’s only active volcano on Barren Island.
  • Lakshadweep: coral atolls in the Arabian Sea, growing coconuts.

Agro-climatic zones#

India’s Planning Commission divided the country into 15 agro-climatic regions (Western Himalaya, Eastern Himalaya, Lower/Middle/Upper/Trans-Gangetic Plains, Eastern Plateau and Hills, Central, Western and Southern Plateau and Hills, East and West Coast Plains, Gujarat Plains and Hills, Western Dry Region, and the Islands) to plan crops by soil and rainfall.
