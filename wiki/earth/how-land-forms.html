How Land Forms: Plates, Mountains and Erosion

Continents drift, collide and tear apart; rain, ice and wind wear them down. The two forces together build every landscape on Earth.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 3 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Introductory

Earth’s land covers about 148.9 million km², roughly 29% of the planet’s surface. Every hill, plain and plateau on it is the result of a tug-of-war between two engines: internal forces (plate tectonics and volcanism) that push land up, and external forces (weathering, rivers, glaciers, wind and waves) that wear it down.

Land in numbers
  • Land area: ~148.9 million km² (29% of Earth’s surface)
  • Highest point: Mount Everest, 8,849 m
  • Lowest dry land: the Dead Sea shore, about −430 m and falling
  • Average land elevation: ~840 m
  • Oldest rocks: ~4.0 billion years (Acasta Gneiss, Canada); oldest mineral grains ~4.4 billion years (Jack Hills zircons, Australia)

Plate tectonics: the engine underneath#

The outer shell of Earth, the lithosphere, is broken into about 15 major plates that float on the hotter, softer asthenosphere. They move 2–10 cm a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. What happens at their edges shapes the land:

The three kinds of plate boundary
- **Convergent (colliding).** When two continents collide, neither sinks, so crust crumples upward. The Indian plate hit Asia around 50 million years ago and is still pushing north about 4–5 cm a year, raising the **Himalaya** and the **Tibetan Plateau**. When ocean crust meets a continent it dives under (subducts), producing volcanic ranges such as the **Andes**. - **Divergent (pulling apart).** Where plates separate, the crust thins, faults and sinks into rift valleys. The **East African Rift** is slowly splitting Africa; in tens of millions of years it may become a new ocean, as the Red Sea already is. - **Transform (sliding past).** Plates grind sideways, building earthquakes rather than mountains, as along California's **San Andreas Fault**.

The supercontinent Pangaea assembled about 335 million years ago and began breaking apart about 200 million years ago. India broke away from Gondwana, crossed the ocean faster than any other continent, and collided with Asia; that is why fossils of the same plants (Glossopteris) and reptiles are found in India, Africa, Antarctica and Australia.

Volcanoes and flood basalts#

Volcanoes build land directly. Some of the largest land features are flood basalts: vast stacks of lava erupted over short geological periods. India’s Deccan Traps covered around 500,000 km² of west-central India about 66 million years ago, close to the time of the dinosaur extinction. Their weathering created India’s fertile black cotton soil. Iceland, Hawaii and the Andaman Sea’s Barren Island are still growing today.

The forces that wear land down#

  1. Weathering breaks rock where it stands: by freezing and thawing (frost wedging), by heating and cooling, by roots, and by chemical reactions with water and carbon dioxide (which dissolves limestone into karst caves and towers).
  2. Rivers carry the debris. The Ganges and Brahmaputra together deliver around a billion tonnes of sediment a year, building the world’s largest delta in Bangladesh and West Bengal.
  3. Glaciers scrape U-shaped valleys, fjords and lakes; the Ice Ages left the lake-studded plains of Canada and Finland.
  4. Wind shapes dunes and carries dust; wind-blown silt (loess) forms some of the most fertile farmland in China, Ukraine and the US Midwest.
  5. Waves cut cliffs, beaches and sea stacks.

Landforms by origin#

Origin Examples
Fold mountains (collision) Himalaya, Alps, Zagros
Volcanic mountains Kilimanjaro, Fuji, Andes
Block mountains and rift valleys Vosges, Black Forest, East African Rift
Plateaus (uplift or lava) Tibetan, Deccan, Colorado, Ethiopian
River plains and deltas Indo-Gangetic Plain, Nile delta, Mekong delta
Glacial landforms Fjords of Norway, Great Lakes, moraines
Aeolian (wind) landforms Sahara ergs, Thar dunes, loess plateaus
Karst Meghalaya caves, Ha Long Bay, Guilin

Why this matters for people#

Landforms decide where rain falls (mountains force air to rise and drop its moisture, leaving a dry rain shadow behind), which soils form, which crops grow and where people live. Most of humanity lives on low river plains and coasts, because flat land with deep alluvial soil and water is the easiest to farm.
