The Green Revolution and Food Security

How high-yielding seeds, irrigation and fertiliser took India from famine to surplus, the problems they created for soil and water, and how the world can feed 10 billion people.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 4 sources🎓 Intermediate

In the mid-1960s India was importing millions of tonnes of wheat under the US PL-480 programme after back-to-back droughts in 1965 and 1966. Within a decade it was self-sufficient in grain. The change is called the Green Revolution.

What changed#

The package
- **High-yielding varieties (HYVs):** semi-dwarf wheat bred by **Norman Borlaug** at CIMMYT in Mexico, and semi-dwarf rice such as **IR8** from IRRI in the Philippines. Short, stiff stems carried heavy grain heads without falling over. - **Irrigation:** canals and, above all, millions of tube wells. - **Chemical fertilisers and pesticides.** - **Policy:** guaranteed minimum support prices (MSP), government procurement (FCI, 1965), credit and agricultural universities (Pantnagar, Ludhiana). In India the effort was led by **M. S. Swaminathan**, agriculture minister **C. Subramaniam** and Prime Ministers Lal Bahadur Shastri and Indira Gandhi.

India’s wheat production rose from about 12 million tonnes (1964–65) to over 110 million tonnes in recent years, and rice from about 39 to over 130 million tonnes. Punjab, Haryana and western UP became the national granary.

The costs#

  • Groundwater: satellites showed northwest India losing about 17.7 km³ of groundwater a year (Rodell et al. 2009), driven by free electricity and water-hungry paddy in a semi-arid region. Water tables in parts of Punjab fall about 0.5–1 m a year.
  • Soil: declining organic carbon, micronutrient (zinc) deficiencies, salinity and waterlogging.
  • Diversity: traditional varieties and millets were displaced; diets shifted toward rice and wheat.
  • Air: stubble burning after the rice harvest adds to north India’s winter smog.
  • Inequality: irrigated regions and larger farmers gained most at first.

Pingali (2012) concluded that the Green Revolution averted famine and spared vast areas of forest from being cleared, but that the next stage must focus on water, soils and nutrition.

Feeding 10 billion#

The world already produces enough calories for everyone, yet about 700 million people go hungry because of poverty, conflict and waste. Researchers (Foley et al. 2011) point to five strategies: stop expanding farmland into forests; close yield gaps; use water and fertiliser more efficiently; shift diets; and cut food waste (about a third of food is lost or wasted). In India, current efforts include crop diversification away from paddy in Punjab, the revival of millets (2023 was the UN International Year of Millets, proposed by India), natural and zero-budget farming trials, micro-irrigation and the Soil Health Card.

Sources: Pingali 2012, PNAS · Rodell et al. 2009, Nature · Foley et al. 2011, Nature · Wikipedia: Green Revolution in India
