For around 300,000 years *Homo sapiens* lived by hunting, fishing and gathering. Then, after the last **Ice Age** ended about **11,700 years ago**, the climate became warmer, wetter and more stable, and in several regions people began to plant and herd. This **Neolithic Revolution** was not a single event but a slow shift over thousands of years.

## The centres of domestication

| Region | When (approx.) | First crops and animals |
|---|---|---|
| **Fertile Crescent** (Iraq, Syria, Turkey, Iran, Levant) | 11,500–10,000 years ago | Emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, chickpeas, flax; sheep, goats, cattle, pigs |
| **China**: Yangtze and Yellow River | ~9,000 years ago | Rice (Yangtze); foxtail and broomcorn millet (Yellow River); pigs, chickens |
| **New Guinea highlands** (Kuk Swamp) | ~7,000–10,000 years ago | Taro, bananas, sugarcane |
| **Mesoamerica** (southern Mexico) | ~9,000 years ago | Maize (from teosinte), squash, beans, chillies |
| **Andes and Amazonia** | ~8,000–10,000 years ago | Potatoes, quinoa, cassava, peanuts; llamas, alpacas, guinea pigs |
| **Eastern North America** | ~5,000 years ago | Sunflower, squash, goosefoot |
| **Sahel and West Africa** | ~5,000–3,000 years ago | Sorghum, pearl millet, African rice, yams, cowpeas |
| **Ethiopian Highlands** | ~4,000+ years ago | Teff, coffee, finger millet, enset |
| **Indian subcontinent** | ~9,000 years ago (Mehrgarh) | Barley, wheat (from the west), zebu cattle; later rice, mung bean, urad, sesame, cotton, pigeon pea |

## India's first farmers

**Mehrgarh** in Balochistan (now Pakistan), settled about **7000 BCE**, is one of the earliest farming villages in South Asia, growing barley and wheat and herding humped **zebu cattle**, which were domesticated locally. Later, sites in the Ganga valley such as **Lahuradewa** show early rice use, and the **Harappan** (Indus) civilisation, from about 2600 BCE, grew wheat, barley, peas, sesame and, famously, some of the earliest **cotton** for textiles. Pigeon pea, mung and urad beans and millets were domesticated in peninsular India.

## How a wild plant becomes a crop

Early farmers unknowingly selected for the **domestication syndrome**:
- seeds that **stay on the plant** instead of shattering (so they can be harvested);
- larger seeds and fruits;
- plants that ripen together;
- less bitterness and fewer toxins;
- loss of seed dormancy, so seeds sprout when sown.

Maize is the extreme case: its wild ancestor **teosinte** has a few hard kernels on a tiny spike.

## Consequences

Farming produced surplus food, permanent villages, rising populations, the first cities, writing, taxes, armies, and specialised crafts. It also brought harder work, poorer early diets, crowd diseases caught from animals (measles, smallpox, influenza) and inequality. Over the following millennia farming transformed about half of the planet's habitable land.
