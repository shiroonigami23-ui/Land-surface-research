Snow, Ice, Glaciers and Permafrost

About a tenth of Earth's land lies under ice and a further quarter of the northern land is frozen underground. How the cryosphere works, why it is shrinking and what that means for water and sea level.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 3 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Intermediate

The cryosphere is every part of Earth where water is frozen: snow, glaciers, ice caps, ice sheets, sea ice, lake and river ice, and frozen ground (permafrost). On land, glaciers and ice sheets cover about 10% of the surface and store about 69% of the world’s fresh water.

Snow#

Snow forms when water vapour freezes directly onto ice nuclei in clouds below 0 °C. Seasonal snow covers up to about a third of Earth’s land in the northern winter. Its brilliant white surface reflects 80–90% of sunlight (high albedo), so less snow means more heat absorbed, a feedback that speeds warming. In the Himalaya, Hindu Kush and Alps, spring snowmelt fills rivers just as farmers plant crops.

Glaciers: rivers of ice#

A glacier forms where more snow falls each winter than melts each summer. Over decades the snow compresses into dense blue ice that flows downhill under its own weight, carving U-shaped valleys and leaving moraines.

The world's ice
  • Antarctic Ice Sheet: ~14 million km², up to 4.8 km thick; enough ice to raise sea level ~58 m
  • Greenland Ice Sheet: ~1.7 million km²; enough to raise sea level ~7 m
  • Mountain glaciers: ~200,000 of them; the Himalaya–Karakoram–Hindu Kush hold the most ice outside the poles, the “Third Pole”
  • India’s longest glaciers include Siachen (~76 km) and Gangotri (~30 km), source of the Bhagirathi/Ganga

Glaciers are retreating fast#

Glaciers lost 408 ± 132 gigatonnes of ice in hydrological year 2025, equal to about 1.1 mm of global sea-level rise, according to the World Glacier Monitoring Service; loss has risen from under 100 Gt a year in 1976–1995 to about 390 Gt a year in 2016–2025. Since 1975 the cumulative loss is about 9,583 Gt, or 26.4 mm of sea-level rise. 2025 was the fourth year in a row in which every one of the world’s 19 glacier regions lost mass (Copernicus).

Why it matters: About 1.9 billion people depend on water from the Hindu Kush Himalaya. In the short run melting raises river flows (“peak water”); in the long run, as glaciers shrink, summer and dry-season flows fall. Glacial lakes swell and can burst (GLOFs): Kedarnath 2013 and South Lhonak in Sikkim 2023 were catastrophic examples.

Permafrost#

Permafrost is ground that stays at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years. It underlies about 15% of the exposed land of the Northern Hemisphere (Siberia, Alaska, northern Canada, the Tibetan Plateau). Arctic permafrost stores roughly 1,400–1,600 billion tonnes of carbon, about twice what is in the atmosphere. As it thaws: - roads, pipelines and buildings tilt and crack; - the ground slumps into thermokarst lakes and craters; - microbes decompose old organic matter and release CO₂ and methane, a feedback that adds to warming.

Tundra and snowy regions#

Above the tree line lies tundra: treeless plains of mosses, lichens, sedges and dwarf shrubs, with a growing season of 50–60 days. It is grazed by reindeer, caribou and musk oxen and herded by peoples such as the Sámi and Nenets. In the Himalaya, the cold deserts of Ladakh and Spiti grow barley, peas and apricots in short summers using glacier-fed channels, and engineer Sonam Wangchuk’s “ice stupas” store winter meltwater as artificial glaciers for spring irrigation.
