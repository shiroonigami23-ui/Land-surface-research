Which Crops Grow Where, and Why

Each crop has a climate, soil and water "comfort zone". How temperature, rainfall, day length and soil decide the world's crop map, with India's kharif, rabi and zaid seasons.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Introductory

A crop grows best where its needs match the land: enough warmth for its season, water at the right time, suitable soil and the right day length. Change any of these and yields drop.

The main needs of major crops#

Crop Temperature Water (per season) Soil Classic regions
Rice 20–35 °C, humid 1,200–2,500 mm (often flooded) Clayey, water-holding Monsoon Asia, deltas, Punjab (irrigated)
Wheat 10–25 °C, cool growth, warm ripening 450–650 mm Well-drained loam Indo-Gangetic plain, Great Plains, Ukraine, Russia
Maize 18–32 °C 500–800 mm Fertile, well-drained US Corn Belt, China, Brazil, Karnataka
Cotton 21–30 °C, 210 frost-free days 700–1,300 mm Black soils Deccan, Gujarat, Texas, Xinjiang
Sugarcane 21–27 °C, long hot season 1,500–2,500 mm Deep loam UP, Maharashtra, Brazil
Tea 13–30 °C, frequent rain 1,500–2,500 mm, well spread Acidic, sloping, drained Assam, Darjeeling, Nilgiris, Kenya, Sri Lanka
Coffee (arabica) 15–24 °C 1,500–2,000 mm Volcanic or laterite, shaded Karnataka, Brazil, Ethiopia, Colombia
Millets 25–35 °C 250–600 mm Light, sandy Rajasthan, Sahel, Deccan

India’s three cropping seasons#

  • Kharif (sown June–July with the monsoon, harvested September–October): rice, maize, cotton, soybeans, groundnut, jowar, bajra, tur, moong, urad, sugarcane (long season).
  • Rabi (sown October–December, harvested March–April, watered by irrigation and winter rain): wheat, barley, mustard, chickpea, lentil, peas.
  • Zaid (short summer season, March–June): watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, fodder, vegetables.

Day length and climate change#

Many crops respond to day length (photoperiod): some wheat and rice varieties flower only when days shorten or lengthen. Green Revolution breeders deliberately made varieties day-neutral so they could grow across latitudes.

With warming, crop belts are shifting: maize and soybeans are spreading north in Canada and Russia, apple orchards in Himachal are moving uphill, and coffee may lose up to half of its suitable land by 2050 in some projections. Heat at flowering is the most damaging; every 1 °C of warming cuts global wheat yields by around 6% (Zhao et al. 2017).
