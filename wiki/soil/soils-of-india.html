Soils of India

India's eight major soil groups (alluvial, black, red, laterite, arid, saline, peaty and forest soils): where they occur, how they formed and what they grow.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Introductory

The Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) classifies India’s soils into eight major groups. Together they explain why Punjab grows wheat, Maharashtra grows cotton, Kerala grows rubber and Rajasthan grows bajra.

Soil Share of India (approx.) Main areas Main crops
Alluvial ~40% Indo-Gangetic plain, deltas, coastal strips Rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute, pulses
Red and yellow ~18% Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand Millets, groundnut, pulses, cotton
Black (regur) ~15% Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Telangana Cotton, soybeans, sorghum, wheat, sugarcane
Arid (desert) ~4% Rajasthan, Gujarat, Haryana Bajra, guar, pulses
Laterite ~3.7% Western Ghats, Kerala, Odisha, Northeast Cashew, tea, coffee, rubber
Saline and alkaline ~1.3% Punjab, Haryana, UP, Gujarat coast, Sundarbans Salt-tolerant rice and barley after reclamation
Peaty and marshy small Kerala (Kuttanad), Sundarbans, Odisha coast Rice
Forest and mountain ~8% Himalaya, Western Ghats Tea, fruit, spices

Alluvial soils#

Laid down by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems and coastal rivers. Khadar (new alluvium) is renewed by floods every year and is very fertile; bhangar (old alluvium) lies above flood level and contains lime nodules (kankar). Rich in potash, often low in nitrogen and phosphorus. These soils feed most of India.

Black cotton soil (regur)#

Formed from the weathering of the Deccan Traps basalt. Rich in clay, calcium, magnesium and potash; poor in nitrogen and phosphorus. It swells when wet and cracks deeply when dry, so it “ploughs itself” by mixing as cracks fill. It holds moisture for months, which suits rain-fed cotton. Hard to work when wet (“sticky”) or very dry.

Red and yellow soils#

Developed on old crystalline rocks (granite, gneiss) in low-rainfall parts of the plateau. The red colour comes from iron oxide; yellow where the iron is hydrated. Porous and light, low in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus; productive with fertiliser and irrigation.

Laterite soils#

From the Latin later, “brick”. Formed under heavy rain and high temperature, which leach away silica and leave iron and aluminium. Acidic and poor, but excellent for tree crops such as cashew, tea, coffee and rubber. Freshly cut laterite hardens in air and has been used as building stone for centuries (temples in Kerala and Odisha, houses in Goa and Konkan).

Arid soils#

Sandy, light-coloured, saline in places, with a calcium carbonate (kankar) layer that blocks water. Low in organic matter. Turn productive with irrigation, as along the Indira Gandhi Canal.

Saline and alkaline soils#

Known locally as reh, kallar or usar. Salt rises with groundwater in poorly drained, over-irrigated land, or is left by seawater on coasts. Reclaimed with gypsum, drainage, salt-tolerant varieties (such as CSR rice from the Central Soil Salinity Research Institute, Karnal) and green manure.

Soil health in India#

The national Soil Health Card scheme, launched in 2015, has tested farmers’ fields for 12 parameters and recommended fertiliser doses. Common problems are low organic carbon (often below 0.5%), nitrogen deficiency, zinc deficiency and fertiliser imbalance (too much urea relative to phosphorus and potash).
