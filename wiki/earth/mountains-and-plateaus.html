Mountains, Mountain Tops and Plateaus

How mountains rise, why their tops are cold and snowy, how altitude creates climate zones stacked like floors of a building, and why plateaus feed and water billions.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 3 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Intermediate

Mountains cover about a quarter of Earth’s land and are home to roughly 1.1 billion people, yet they supply between half and most of the world’s fresh water, earning them the name “water towers”. Plateaus, high flat tablelands, include some of the most farmed and mined land on Earth.

How mountains rise#

Four ways to build a mountain
- **Fold mountains** form where plates collide and crust buckles: the Himalaya, Alps, Atlas and the ancient Appalachians and Aravallis. - **Volcanic mountains** are piles of erupted lava and ash: Kilimanjaro, Fuji, Mauna Kea, Elbrus. - **Fault-block mountains** are slabs of crust pushed up along faults, like the Sierra Nevada in California. - **Dome and residual mountains** are bulges of rock or the hard remains of older ranges worn down by erosion, such as the Nilgiris' older rocks or the Ozarks.

Young ranges are tall and jagged (the Himalaya is still rising ~4 mm a year at Everest). Old ranges like the Aravalli (over 1.5 billion years) are worn down to rounded hills.

Mountain tops: life at the edge#

Air cools by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m you climb (the environmental lapse rate), and holds less oxygen: at Everest’s summit air pressure is about a third of sea level. As a result mountain slopes are stacked into altitudinal zones that mirror the trip from equator to pole:

Zone (Himalaya, approx.) Elevation Vegetation and land use
Tropical/subtropical < 1,000 m Sal forest, rice, tea in the foothills
Temperate 1,000–3,000 m Oak, rhododendron, apples, potatoes, terraced fields
Subalpine 3,000–4,000 m Fir, birch, juniper; summer pastures (bugyals)
Alpine 4,000–5,000 m Meadows, cushion plants, yak grazing
Nival (snow) > 5,000 m Permanent snow, glaciers, bare rock

The snow line, above which snow lasts all year, sits at ~5,000–6,000 m in the Himalaya, ~3,000 m in the Alps and at sea level near the poles. Rising temperatures are pushing snow lines and tree lines upward, shrinking the alpine zone and its specialised species.

Mountains make weather#

When moist wind meets a range it rises, cools and drops rain or snow on the windward side (orographic rainfall). The other side lies in a dry rain shadow. Examples: - The Western Ghats catch the southwest monsoon, giving the Konkan and Malabar coasts 2,500–5,000 mm a year, while Pune, just inland, gets ~700 mm. - The Himalaya block cold Central Asian air from India and wring the monsoon dry, leaving Ladakh and Tibet as cold deserts. - The Andes create the Atacama on one side (with the cold Humboldt Current) and drench the Amazon slope on the other.

Plateaus#

Great plateaus
  • Tibetan Plateau: ~2.5 million km², average ~4,500 m; source of the Indus, Ganges’ tributaries, Brahmaputra, Yangtze, Yellow, Mekong and Salween rivers. Holds the largest ice outside the poles, sometimes called the “Third Pole”.
  • Deccan Plateau: most of peninsular India, tilted east so its rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) flow to the Bay of Bengal. Black soil in the northwest, red soil in the south and east.
  • Chota Nagpur Plateau: India’s richest mineral belt (coal, iron ore, mica, bauxite).
  • Altiplano: ~3,750 m in Bolivia and Peru, home of Lake Titicaca and potato and quinoa domestication.
  • Ethiopian Highlands: “roof of Africa”, source of the Blue Nile; teff and coffee originated here.
  • Colorado Plateau: carved into the Grand Canyon.

Pressures on mountains#

  • Glacier loss: glaciers lost 408 ± 132 gigatonnes of ice in 2025 alone, and loss has accelerated from under 100 Gt a year in 1976–1995 to about 390 Gt a year in 2016–2025 (WGMS). That threatens the meltwater many Asian rivers rely on in the dry season.
  • Glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), such as the 2013 Kedarnath and 2023 Sikkim floods.
  • Landslides made worse by road cutting and deforestation, e.g. Joshimath’s subsidence (2023) and the Wayanad debris flows (2024).
