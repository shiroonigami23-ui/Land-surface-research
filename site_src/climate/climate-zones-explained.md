**Weather** is what happens today; **climate** is the average over at least 30 years. Climates differ because of five controls: **latitude** (how directly the Sun shines), **altitude**, **distance from the sea**, **ocean currents**, and **prevailing winds and pressure belts**, plus mountains that block or lift those winds.

## The Köppen classification

Proposed by the German-Russian climatologist **Wladimir Köppen** in 1884 and refined with Rudolf Geiger, it groups climates by temperature and rainfall, because vegetation responds to both. Each climate has a letter code:

| Group | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **A** Tropical | Every month averages above 18 °C | Af rainforest (Kerala hills, Amazon), Am monsoon (Mumbai coast), Aw savanna (most of peninsular India) |
| **B** Dry | Evaporation exceeds rainfall | BWh hot desert (Thar), BWk cold desert (Ladakh), BSh semi-arid (interior Deccan) |
| **C** Temperate | Coldest month 0–18 °C | Csa Mediterranean, Cfa humid subtropical, Cwa monsoon-influenced subtropical (Delhi, Lucknow), Cfb oceanic (London) |
| **D** Continental | Coldest month below 0 °C, warmest above 10 °C | Dfa/Dfb (Ukraine, US Midwest), Dfc subarctic (Siberia) |
| **E** Polar | Warmest month below 10 °C | ET tundra, EF ice cap |
| **H** Highland | Varies with altitude | Himalaya, Andes |

Second and third letters show rainfall timing (f = wet all year, s = dry summer, w = dry winter, m = monsoon) and summer heat (a = hot, b = warm, c = cool, d = very cold winter).

## Global circulation in one picture

Sunlight heats the equator most. Hot air rises there (the rainy **Intertropical Convergence Zone, ITCZ**), spreads poleward, sinks around 30° (dry deserts), and returns as the **trade winds**. Further out, westerlies bring storms to the mid-latitudes, and cold air sinks at the poles. The ITCZ shifts north and south with the seasons, and this movement, amplified by land heating, drives the **monsoons**.

## Climate zones are moving

A 1-km Köppen map by Beck and colleagues (2018) projected that by 2071–2100, under high emissions, a large share of land would shift to a different main climate class: tropical and dry zones expand, while polar and cold zones shrink. Farmers already see it: wine grapes now grow in southern England, apple growing in Himachal is moving uphill, and Mediterranean summers are drier.
